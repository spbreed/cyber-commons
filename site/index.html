What is Cyber Commons

A commons for the knowledge that keeps everyone safe.

A commons is a shared resource no one owns and everyone may draw from — a village green, a body of open research. Cyber Commons is that, for the skills of defending in the age of AI.

We are the first generation of defenders whose attackers compound — and the knowledge to answer them is still being locked up.

Cyber Commons is a free, open training programme covering the two halves of the field: using AI to do security work, and securing the AI systems you now depend on. Twelve tracks organised by the job you actually hold, 104 sessions, every one shipping commands that really run.

It is built deliberately on open-source infrastructure and open-weight models — CNCF and Linux Foundation projects for the control plane, Llama, Kimi and GLM for the intelligence. Not because open is cheaper, but because a commons that requires a frontier-lab account is not a commons. Everything here runs on the laptop you already own.

The two halves, never blurred

AI for Security
Pointing agents at the work: triaging alerts, reviewing code, hunting bugs, drafting exploits in a sealed lab, shipping validated patches — and measuring whether any of it is actually reliable.
Security of AI
Defending what you just built: the models, agents, tool schemas and non-human identities are a fresh attack surface with failure modes your existing playbook cannot express.
Why both, always
A practitioner taught only one half gets surprised. The team that automates triage without governing the agent doing it has simply moved the risk somewhere it can't see.

Why now: the defensive knowledge gap is widening

AI capability is compounding — each generation of model makes the next cheaper to build, and agentic systems now act, not just answer. Offense feels that curve first, because attacking needs one working path and defending needs all of them closed.

What scales almost freely
  • Autonomous discovery — agents that read code and find real, novel vulnerabilities
  • Exploit generation at the speed of a build pipeline
  • Campaigns without headcount — no team required to run many at once
  • One path is enough — the attacker needs a single opening to succeed
What does not scale by itself
  • Judgement — knowing which finding is real, and which confident answer is a hallucination
  • Trust in your own tools — a probabilistic scanner is worthless unmeasured
  • Control-plane design — identity, delegation, containment, stop authority
  • Every path must hold — the defender has to close all of them
That asymmetry is why hoarding defensive knowledge is now actively dangerous. Offensive capability diffuses on its own — it is defensive capability that has to be deliberately spread. If only the best-funded teams learn to direct, doubt and prove these systems, the gap between them and the hospital, the school district, the two-person NGO becomes the softest target in the network we all share.

Navigating cyber singularity

"Singularity" here is not a prophecy about machines waking up. It is the practical point where systems act faster than the humans nominally supervising them can follow — where an agent's decision-to-action loop closes in milliseconds and your review cycle takes a day. Parts of security are already there.

You cannot navigate that by reading about it. You navigate it by building the instincts and the instrumentation while the curve is still climbing: bounded autonomy, attenuated delegation, containment you have actually tested, evaluation you can put in front of a regulator. That is the whole curriculum, and it is free because it has to be.

The commons model

Free isn't a promotion. It's the whole point.

A commons is knowledge no one owns and everyone can draw from. Here's why this one stays open — and how it stays current in a field that turns over every week.

Why it stays free

  • Hoarded security knowledge makes everyone less safe. Shared defense is stronger defense.
  • The under-resourced can't wait for budget. NGOs, students and small teams face the same attackers as banks.
  • No frontier-lab account required. Every lab runs on open-weight models — Llama, Kimi, GLM — locally or on a free tier.

How it stays current

  • Tracked against living sources — OWASP LLM & Agentic Top 10, MITRE ATLAS, NIST AI RMF and CNCF releases.
  • Open like the tools it teaches. Chapters and labs are corrected by pull request; the curriculum is versioned in the open.
  • Every lab actually executes — commands that run against real targets, with real output.

The core idea

Two disciplines the field keeps calling one thing.

"Cyber AI" is really two jobs that need different skills, different tools, and different instincts. Every track teaches both — a track that only teaches one produces a practitioner who gets surprised.

Direction 01 — Defend with AI

AI for Security

Turn AI into your force multiplier across the whole security lifecycle. Fewer manual hours, faster response — if you can trust the output.

  • Agentic SOC triage, threat hunting & alert correlation and deduplication
  • Architecture, code-review, SAST & DAST agents
  • SCA for OSS libraries + reverse-engineering with decompilers
  • Pentest & exploit-generation agents, run in a sealed lab
  • Rapid patching agents that validate the fix before the PR
  • Writing eval harnesses — pass@k vs pass^k reliability
Direction 02 — Defend the AI

Security of AI

Everything you build with AI becomes something an attacker builds against. Govern the models, agents and identities you now depend on.

  • Agentic security & MCP — why a tool schema is a trust boundary
  • Prompt injection, direct & indirect, and real defenses
  • Guardrails — operating vs outcome, layered
  • NHI governance, delegation chains & the agent gateway
  • Secure open-weight supply chain, model cards, data privacy
  • Human-in-the-loop, legal/compliance, 3P/4P data risk
How it works

A new way to learn security — because the old one can't keep up.

Reading about a control doesn't build the instinct to ship it. Every session is the same shape: see it drawn, watch it run, then run it yourself from a repo you keep.

METHOD / 01

Lightboard, not lecture

The concept is drawn out on a lightboard so the mental model lands before any code does. You leave understanding the why, not just copying the how.

METHOD / 02

Live demo, real execution

Then we run it — an actual agent against an actual target, open-weight model and all. No slides of screenshots; the thing works on screen, and it works on your machine.

METHOD / 03

A repo you keep

Every lab ships as source in an open GitHub repo. Clone it, break it, adapt it to your own stack. The commons hands you the tools, not just the knowledge.

Who it's for

Five functions, twelve chairs — you stay in yours, the job underneath changes.

This isn't a ladder you climb from analyst to CISO. It's the org chart you already work in. You stay a pentester. You stay a GRC analyst. What changes is what the work is made of.

FUNCTION A
Architecture

Decide what's possible

Architect, IAM/NHI engineer, platform security. You design the control plane other people's agents run inside.

FUNCTION B
AppSec

Meet agents first

AppSec reviewers and the new harness-engineer role. Closest to developers, so first to meet agents at scale.

FUNCTION C
Offensive

Find what's true

Pentesters and researchers. You get a whole new target class — the agent itself — and you test at L3.

FUNCTION D
SecOps

Run at machine speed

SOC, detection and IR. You stop reading alerts and start compressing signal — and investigate agents as actors.

FUNCTION E
GRC / CISO

Make it defensible

GRC, regulatory and the CISO office. You hold promotion criteria, stop authority, and the board narrative.

Local
Ollama or llama.cpp — pull a quantised Llama 3.3, GLM or Kimi variant and run it on a laptop. No account, no egress, works offline. The default for every lab.
Free tiers
Open-weight models are hosted free by several providers on rate-limited tiers (and on Hugging Face). Good when your laptop can't hold the bigger model. Limits change — check current terms before you rely on one.
Self-host
vLLM on one borrowed GPU serves a whole class. LiteLLM puts one OpenAI-compatible endpoint in front of whatever you chose, so labs never hard-code a provider.
Never required
Commercial frontier models are named where relevant but never required. Every lab's acceptance criteria are reachable on open weights.

Minimum hardware: a laptop. Nothing here needs a credit card or a frontier-lab account.

Why it matters

Agents don't just change the tools. They change the engineer.

The instinct that made a great cyber engineer for twenty years is quietly becoming the wrong one. The job isn't doing the work by hand anymore — it's directing work you have to be able to trust, doubt, and prove.

Retrain the reflex, not just the résumé. That's the whole point of the commons.
