Overview

The problem

Somebody signed off on the platform when it was a chatbot. It now moves money, ships code and reads contracts, and nothing in the approval process noticed. A control tested six months ago is not passing — it is unevidenced — and that third state is invisible to most tooling, which is why a register can be entirely green and entirely unsupported.

The solution

Build the indicator set: an inventory for the denominator, risk tiering for the target, control mapping for the subject, owners by name, and verification that re-reads rather than citing last year. Then point the same indicators outward at regulators, and run them as a programme sequenced by distance from target.

How you know it worked

The unit is the key control indicator: computed from the estate, with a denominator, and a target written before the measurement. F1.13 computes six of them against the CyberTravels repository and returns five gaps and one pass — the pass being what proves the instrument discriminates rather than simply complaining.

▦The idea the whole course hangs off

Read this before the first lesson. A taught session opens with it; alone, read it and move on.

Three chapters: build the framework, build the platform, run the programme. F1 turns framework controls into indicators somebody can compute. F2 maps those outward to the regulations that apply. F3 is the CISO office — sequencing, org design, metrics, and how to say no.

F1.1 is the hinge of the whole function and every F page anchors back to it: from framework control to key control indicator. A control in a framework is a sentence. An indicator is a number with a date on it, computed from an artefact, which is the only form in which a control survives supervision.

The direction of mapping is the argument, and it runs one way only: controls outward to frameworks, never frameworks inward to controls. A programme built per framework builds the same control three times and evidences it none. F2.3 picks the framework that covers the most of your controls as a spine and supplies the remainder from the others.

◉Pitfalls, and what to do

PitfallWhat to doIf you are running it for a room
Mapping frameworks inward to controlsIt builds the same control three times and evidences it none. F2.3 is the lesson: your controls are the spine, frameworks are the labels.This is the derail that costs the most time in a mixed room.
Treating a control as operating because it existsF1.6 separates operating from watched. A control is operating when something enforces it, not when something documents it.—
Reporting a control as evidenced against behaviour that has changedF1.7 and F1.9: a retrain, a fine-tune or a re-index invalidates a reading. The control has not failed — it is unevidenced, which is a different and more honest state.—
Answering a regulator with coverage instead of accuracyF2.9 rehearses the three openings that actually get used. Conformance and expert accuracy get reported separately or not at all.—
Reading F3 as the soft chapterF3.3's sequencing and F3.7's build curve are the two lessons that decide whether a programme has control coverage or demos at the end of a year.—
